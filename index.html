Renewing the Mandir: Two Reforms for a Living Hindu Tradition

By Raj Chaturvedi

I want to argue for two changes in how Hindu worship is run. Neither touches what Hindus believe. Both are about who stands in front of the deity and in what language the prayer is understood.

The first is that more young people, and women, should become priests, with older priests handing over in an orderly way instead of working until they can’t. The second is that Hinduism should build a living English-language literature of prayer, not only translations, and have priests who can explain a ritual in English as easily as they chant it in Sanskrit.

My claim is that a tradition can change who carries it, and how it is understood, without changing what it is. Hinduism has done this before, more than once.

Why any of this matters

Science can tell me how a cell divides. It can’t tell me why a life is worth living or what to say at a funeral. People have always taken those questions to religion, and sociologists have spent a century trying to explain why it works.

Émile Durkheim argued that shared ritual binds a group together. Max Weber argued that religion answers the problem of suffering, the question of why bad things happen, in a way raw facts never will. Peter Berger called religion a “sacred canopy”, a shared frame that makes the world feel livable. And Robert Putnam and David Campbell found, in American data, that regular worshippers volunteer more, give more and trust their neighbours more.

What I take from all this is that religion isn’t only an idea in someone’s head. It is people, places, words and routines. Routines age. If the people running them never change, and the language no longer matches how the young think, nothing collapses. The young simply stop coming, slowly, and nobody notices until a festival looks thin.

Reform one: pass the lamp

In many places the pattern is the same. The priest is elderly, the work pays little, the status is lower than a career in engineering or medicine, and the way in often depends on who your father was. A young person looking at that makes the sensible choice and goes elsewhere. So the number of trained priests shrinks while the number of rituals people want performed stays about the same. I think this is the most practical threat to temple life, bigger than any argument about doctrine.

Now the uncomfortable part. If the old are to make room, someone has to say so. I don’t mean pushing anyone out. Hindu thought already has the idea built in: after the householder years comes vanaprastha, the stage where you stop running things and start guiding. Put that into a temple and it looks like this. A chief priest serves up to a set age, then becomes a respected mentor with a pension. A young priest trains beside him for years before taking over. Selection happens by examination in ritual, mantra, scripture and conduct, and anyone who passes can sit it. The elder’s knowledge gets handed on instead of being buried with him.

Tamil Nadu is the nearest thing to a real experiment. The state’s temple department has run priest-training schools and appointed trained priests from outside the traditional hereditary groups, and in 2023 three women finished training in the Vaishnava Pancharatra tradition at a school run by the Srirangam temple. It has been contested, and temples that follow strict agama texts have their own rules, which courts have taken seriously. Still, it shows that a trained, open route into the priesthood can exist.

On women, I don’t think this is a borrowed Western idea. The Upanishads give us Gargi and Maitreyi arguing philosophy with sages, and the Rigveda credits hymns to women such as Ghosha and Lopamudra. Women already serve as priests in many village shrines, and in the Arya Samaj they conduct rites. We worship Shakti, Lakshmi, Saraswati and Durga in the sanctum, so it is strange to say a woman can’t serve there. And it’s simple arithmetic: half of all Hindus are women, and a priesthood closed to them throws away half its possible talent.

What would we gain? Continuity first: fewer shrines and rites left with nobody qualified. Then connection. A priest who can explain a ritual in the words a college student actually uses gives that student a reason to come back. And confidence in public life. A community led by educated young people can run schools, clinics and cultural programmes out of its temples, which is the kind of civic work Putnam found religious groups doing well.

There’s one claim I find tempting and have decided to be careful with: that young priests could counter extremist ideologies, what some call “urban Naxalism”. The term is disputed. Supporters use it for networks sympathetic to armed insurgency, and critics say it gets stuck on peaceful dissent. I’d rather make the claim I can defend. A religious community with articulate, well-read young leaders is better placed to join public debate on its own terms, and its youth are less likely to feel that the only people talking about their tradition are hostile or ill-informed.

Three conditions matter. Standards have to go up, not down, because opening the door means nothing if the exam is soft. Each sampradaya, whether Shaiva, Vaishnava, Shakta or Smarta, should have its own scholars lead the change instead of having it imposed. And the job has to pay. Nobody raises a family on a pujari’s uncertain income, so a fair salary, a pension and insurance are part of the reform itself.

Reform two: a second language for prayer

Hindu worship happens in Sanskrit, and also in Hindi, Tamil, Telugu, Kannada, Malayalam, Marathi, Bengali and many other languages. Each has produced its own devotional writing. English is the language hundreds of millions of Indians study and work in, and the main language of a large Hindu diaspora, yet it has very little of its own: few ritual guides written in English from the start, little original devotional poetry, and very few priests who can work comfortably in it.

You can see the cost abroad. Community reports and studies describe the same arc. The first generation prays easily in Sanskrit or a mother tongue, the second turns up but can’t follow what is being said, and the third keeps the festivals and the food and drifts from the worship. Temple leaders in North America have raised this for years, and some temples now print English explanations and run classes. Reports from Britain describe temples struggling to hire priests because clergy there are expected to speak English, so the demand isn’t hypothetical. Even inside India, a student schooled in English often meets the Gita through a translation of uncertain quality.

My argument is short. People engage with what they understand. More and more Hindus think and read in English. Language carries identity but isn’t the identity itself, so adding one doesn’t take another away.

History backs this up. The Alvars and Nayanars sang in Tamil. Dnyaneshwar gave the Gita to Marathi readers. Kabir, Mirabai and Tulsidas took devotion into everyday speech, and Tulsidas’s Ramcharitmanas in Awadhi became for millions the best-loved version of the Ramayana. Each time, someone worried the sacred would be thinned out. Each time the tradition got wider and, I’d say, deeper.

English is already part of that story. Vivekananda spoke in English at the Parliament of the World’s Religions in Chicago in 1893 and changed how many people saw Hinduism. Aurobindo and Radhakrishnan wrote philosophy in English that people still read. Tagore’s Gitanjali, devotional poetry in his own English, helped win him the Nobel Prize. English isn’t a wall around Hinduism. Hindus have been using it as a window for well over a century.

What I’m asking for goes beyond translation. A word-for-word version of a shloka usually loses the rhythm and the feeling, and what’s left reads like a legal notice. The alternative I’d call transcreation: English versions made with real literary care and printed beside the Sanskrit, plus original English hymns, poetry and commentary by Hindu writers. In practice that means bilingual ritual guides with the Sanskrit, a transliteration and a clear English explanation of each step. It means priest-training schools that teach English as part of the course. And it means priests who can chant in Sanskrit and then tell you in English what they just did. Diaspora programmes that combine Sanskrit, ritual and English training have started to appear, which suggests the need is already felt.

None of this replaces Sanskrit. Many traditions hold that the sound of a mantra matters, and keeping that pronunciation exact is one of the great achievements of Indian oral culture. English goes alongside it, in explanation, in congregational prayer and in writing.

What stays and what changes

Both reforms follow one rule, and it also answers the fear of becoming “too westernized”: protect the core, renew the carrier. The core is the deity, the mantra, the ritual structure and each sampradaya’s own rules. The carrier is who gets trained and how, and the language people use to understand what is happening in front of them.

Westernization would mean borrowing foreign content or values without thinking. These reforms do the reverse. They use a modern, global language and open, merit-based training to deliver Hindu content better. The test I’d apply to any proposal is simple. Does it leave the sacred core intact while making the tradition easier to enter and more likely to last? If yes, that’s renewal. If it touches the core, it needs the consent and scholarship of the tradition itself.

What the other side says

I’d be fooling myself if I pretended there were no good objections.

Some say hereditary training keeps exacting knowledge alive, and that families who have passed on recitation for generations carry something hard to replace. That’s true, and the reform doesn’t ask anyone to give it up. It asks for equally rigorous training for others, and for real apprenticeship so existing expertise is passed on.

Others point out that many temples follow detailed agama texts about who may officiate, and that courts have treated those rules seriously. Which is why change should go temple by temple, led by each tradition’s own scholars. Some will go further and say the rules themselves should be reopened. That argument is legitimate too.

A third worry is state control. Many Hindus believe temples should be run by their own communities and not by government departments, and they note that other faiths manage their institutions independently. A reform led by temple trusts, mathas and sampradayas answers that better than a state programme would.

Age brings wisdom, someone will say. Yes, and that’s why the elders stay on as mentors.

On English, the fear is that every shift in language chips away at identity. The record from the Alvars to Vivekananda points the other way, as long as Sanskrit and the regional languages stay central.

And on women as priests, devout Hindus differ. Some hold that certain roles have been male by custom. Others point to Vedic women seers, to women priests in folk and reform traditions, and to the fact that Hindu practice has never been uniform. That debate will be settled inside the tradition, not by an essay.

None of these objections kills the reforms. They do tell us how to carry them out: slowly, with scholars, with consent from within each tradition, and with high standards.

Closing

A tradition isn’t kept alive by freezing it. It survives when each new generation can get in, understand what it finds, and eventually take charge. Opening the priesthood to trained young people and to women, and giving Hindu worship a rich voice in English, widens the doorway without moving the temple.

Pass the lamp carefully, let people see its light in more than one language, and the flame is exactly what it always was.
